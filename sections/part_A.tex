\section{Part 1: Math GPT }

\subsection{1.1: Build an appropriate dataset}
 Designing an appropriate dataset is crucial for the performance of the Math GPT model. The dataset was generated via a python script that deterministically creates unique arithmetic problems and their respective solutions. This ensures all ground truth labels are completely accurate. The dataset includes a variety of operations such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division, with two levels of complexity selected via the `--basic` and `--advanced` flags.

 \subsubsection{Basic Expressions}
 The --basic flag generates expressions involving single-digit operands and simple operator combinations. 
 This mode was used during early experimentation to validate that the transformer model, tokenisation method, and evaluation scripts were all functioning as expected.

 \subsubsection{Advanced Expressions}
 The --advanced flag extends this setup to more challenging two-digit arithmetic, including larger operand ranges and higher-magnitude outputs. This mode is intended to test the model's ability to generalise to more complex mathematical tasks, and is used for final evaluation.

The dataset is then split into training and test sets using holdout validation. This ensures that evaluation is performed on unseen expressions, and provides a fair and reliable measure of model performance. Note, one pitfall of this design is that the division expression is under-represented. This is due to generating integer-only results, which significantly reduces the number of valid division expressions that can be generated. This constraint was added to avoid decimal results, which would add sinificant and disproportionate complexity to the division operation.


\subsection{1.2: Define appropriate evaluation metrics}
To evaluate the performance of the Math GPT model, multiple metrics were recorded to capture different aspects of the model's performance. The primary metric used was accuracy, defined as the percentage of correctly predicted expressions in the test set. This is the most task-appropriate metric, as the goal is to have the model produce exact matches to the ground truth solutions.

To provide further granulaity and localisation to this metric, operation-wise accuracy was also recorded. This breaks down the overall accuracy by arithmetic operation (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division), revealing wheter performance is unifrom across operations, or whether the model finds certain operations more challenging.

The Character Error Rate (CER) was implemented retrospectively as results showed predictions were often off by just one or two digits. This metric captures how many single-character edits are required to transform the model output into the correct answer, meaning: CER focuses on near-miss errors, such as predicting 1025 instead of 1024, as opposed to complete hallucinations. 

Finally, the Mean Absolute Error (MAE) measures the average absolute difference between the predicted and true numeric values. This provides insight to how far incorrect predictions deviate from the ground truth.

Using the above string and numerical based metrics in conjunction, provides a balanced and reliable evaluation framework for this assignment.

\subsection{1.4: Explore results for a range of arithmetic options}

\subsubsection{Addition}
Addition is the best-learned operation, achieving an accuracy of 83.94\%. This strong performance could be due to the relatively local and regular structure of addition. Multi-digit addition can commonly be decomposed into digit-wise operations with short dependency chains, where carries typically affect only adjacent digits. For example, expressions such as 12+13=25 are frequently solved correctly. However, as the complexity of the addition increases, such as in 98+76=174, the model struggles more, likely due to the longer dependency chains and carry operations involved.

\subsubsection{Subtraction}
Subtraction achieves the second-highest accuracy at 77.98\%. This sligthly weaker performance is expected, given the added complexity of borrowing compared to carrying, as well as sign handling for negative results. The model performs well on simpler cases such as 54-20=34, but errors are more common when multiple borrow operations or negative results are involved, for example in expressions like 23-89.

\subsection{Division}
Division achieves an accuracy of 62.26\%, which is notably lower than addition and subtraction but still demonstrates non-trivial learning. As mentioned, division is under-represented in the dataset, with only 53 division examples appearing in the test set, compared to roughly one thousand examples for each of the other operators. Typical correctly solved cases include expressions such as 48/6=8 or 81/9=9. Given the small sample size, this accuracy estimate is noisier and less reliable, and performance would be expected to degrade sharply if non-integer quotients or decimal representations were introduced.

\subsubsection{Multiplication}
Multiplication is the weakest operation by a large margin, with an accuracy of just 13.51\%. This behaviour is expected and highlights a fundamental limitation of the model. Multi-digit multiplication requires quadratic interactions between digits, meaning every digit in operand A interacts with every digit in operand B. In addition, long dependency chains due to multiple carries, and the generation of comparatively long outputs complicaates this operation significantly. For example, in cases such as 53*71=3763, the model must correctly generate multiple digits in sequence, and a single incorrect carry can corrupt the entire result. Results show, the model often produces outputs that are numerically incorrect but with near-miss predictions that differ by one or two digits. For this reason, the CER metric was used.

\subsubsection{CER and MAE}
The character error rate of 0.2290 indicates that, on average, roughly 23\% of characters in the model's predicted answers differ from the ground-truth output. This reflects errors at the character level rather than purely numeric correctness. Multiplication is expected to dominate this metric, as its longer outputs make it especially sensitive to cascading mistakes. This would be the first expansion point for future work, creating more detailed analysis on CER, such as per-operation, and reducing character-level errors to improve overall accuracy.

The mean absolute error of 116.94 highlights a similar asymmetry across operations. Errors in addition and subtraction typically produce small numeric deviations, whereas multiplication errors often lead to large numerical discrepancies. A single incorrect digit in a higher place value can result in errors of hundreds or more, causing multiplication to disproportionately inflate MAE despite being only one category of operation.
